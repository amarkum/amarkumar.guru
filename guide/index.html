What to do in the interview

This is roughly how the hour will go, what to say along the way, and a few things to avoid. Read it the day before and skim it again on the morning of the interview.

How the hour usually goes

  • 0–5 min
    Intros. Keep it to about a minute. Say what you do now, one project you owned, and what changed because of it, with a number if you can. Then stop and let them lead.
  • 5–15 min
    Leadership Principles. You'll usually get one or two “tell me about a time…” questions. Tell the story in order: what was going on, what you had to do, what you did, and how it turned out. They'll often ask follow-ups like “what would you do differently?”
  • 15–25 min
    Requirements. The question might be vague on purpose, like “design a logging framework”. That's normal. Ask questions, write down what the system needs to do and how well it needs to do it, and note any assumptions you're making.
  • 25–50 min
    Design. LLD: entities → classes → interfaces → core code. HLD: API → diagram → data model → deep dive. Expect them to add something halfway through, like “now add peak-hour pricing” or “how would you find the nearest store?”. That's part of the test, not a sign you're doing badly.
  • 50–55 min
    Follow-ups. They'll poke at the edges: what if two people do this at once, what if something fails, what if traffic grows 10×, how would you add a new feature.
  • 55–60 min
    Your questions. Always have one or two questions ready. There are some ideas below.

LLD vs HLD: what they check

LLD (class design + code)

  • Requirement gathering, then the class structure explained
  • Design patterns where they fit: Strategy, Factory, Observer, State, Composite/Specification, Builder, Chain of Responsibility
  • Effective Java: immutability, builders, interfaces over classes, enums, composition over inheritance, equals/hashCode, Optional over null
  • Readable, running code with few bugs
  • Handling follow-ups by extending, not rewriting

HLD (architecture)

  • Requirements and rough numbers
  • API design and data model
  • Diagram: clients, LB, services, cache, DB, queues, CDN
  • Deep dive on the hardest part, with trade-offs
  • Scale, consistency, failure handling, monitoring

Say this

  • “Can I take a minute to clarify requirements?”
  • “I'll assume X. Tell me if that's wrong.”
  • “I'll use a Strategy here so new pricing rules don't change existing code.”
  • “Two users could hit this at once, so I'll make the update atomic.”
  • “The trade-off is X vs Y. Given our requirements I'd pick X.”
  • “If this service fails, here's what happens…”
  • “I haven't used that, but here's how I'd reason about it.”
  • In stories: “I decided… I built… the result was 30% faster.”

Avoid this

  • Jumping into code before requirements
  • Long silences. Think out loud instead
  • Naming tools without a reason (“just use Kafka”)
  • “We did it” in stories without saying what you did
  • Bluffing about something you don't know
  • Arguing with hints. Hints are help
  • Saying “that's easy” or rushing
  • Criticising your current team or company
  • Bringing up salary or other offers in this round

What she is likely to ask

She is an SDE III at Amazon. SDE IIIs run a hands-on round: a design problem close to their own work, sharp follow-ups, and 1–2 Leadership Principles. Below is what she is most likely to ask in that round — not a guess about her level. More on the Know your interviewer page.

Most likely design questions, in order

  1. Payments or wallet flow: charge, refund, retry safely. Expect idempotency, PENDING → SUCCESS/FAILED, a double-entry ledger, reconciliation, and “what if the bank times out?”
  2. Something from her own team (custom problem): billing, invoicing, seller payouts, or syncing money/status between services. Clarify requirements carefully; she'll know every edge case.
  3. LLD with code: rate limiter, notification router, parking lot with peak pricing, file search with filters, or a shipping cost calculator. Then “add this feature” — extend, don't rewrite.
  4. AWS-shaped follow-up: DynamoDB vs SQL? SQS vs SNS? How do you make a worker idempotent? What lives in S3 vs the DB?
  5. Concurrency: “Two requests at once — last unit or same payment. What happens?”
  6. Operations: “How would you monitor this? What alarms? How do you roll back?”

Likely Leadership Principles questions

  • Ownership: “Tell me about a time you owned a problem outside your scope.”
  • Dive Deep: “Tell me about a hard bug or production issue you debugged.”
  • Customer Obsession: “A time you changed a design because of customer impact.”
  • Deliver Results / Bias for Action: “A tight deadline, what did you cut and why?”
  • Earn Trust / Have Backbone: “A time you disagreed with a senior engineer.”
  • Learn and Be Curious: “A time you leveled someone up, or learned a new domain fast.”

How to play it

  • Talk to her like a teammate you're reviewing a design with. It's fine to ask, “Is this close to what your team works on?”
  • With money, say early that getting it right matters more than being fast, and bring up retries and duplicate payments before she has to ask.
  • If you mention an AWS service, say why you picked it. Keep your code tidy and your names clear. Senior engineers notice that.

Common follow-ups and how to answer

  • “Add this new feature.” Show it's a new class implementing an existing interface (Open/Closed).
  • “Two users at once?” Atomic conditional update, lock, or optimistic version check. Name which and why.
  • “Find the nearest X.” Geohash or quadtree, search own cell plus neighbours (how Uber does it).
  • “What if it's retried?” Idempotency key.
  • “What if this goes down?” Replicas, retries with backoff, fallback, dead-letter queue, alarms.
  • “How do you know it's healthy?” Metrics (latency, error rate), dashboards, alarms, logs with request IDs.

Questions to ask at the end

  • What does the team own in FinTech, and what's the hardest problem right now?
  • What does a great first six months look like for this role?
  • How does the team handle on-call and operational load?
